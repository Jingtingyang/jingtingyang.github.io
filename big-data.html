Can Machine Learning Help Investors Pick Better Stocks?

I tested whether patterns hidden in financial data could help investors make better stock-selection decisions, not just better predictions.

01 / THE QUESTION

Thousands of stocks. Which ones are worth choosing?

Investors have access to huge amounts of information about companies: prices, past returns, profitability, valuation and much more. But having more data does not automatically lead to better decisions.

My research asked a practical question: can machine learning identify useful signals from company data and turn those signals into an investment strategy with attractive returns relative to its risks?

“

The goal was not simply to predict stock returns. It was to test whether those predictions could improve investment decisions.

02 / THE APPROACH

From raw data to an investment decision

I designed a four-step process that resembles how an investment research team might evaluate a new strategy.

01▦

Gather the evidence

Organise historical US stock data and company characteristics into five sets, ranging from 20 to 100 indicators.

02✳

Train three models

Compare LASSO, Random Forest and XGBoost to estimate which stocks may perform better or worse.

03↗

Build a portfolio

Use model rankings to form a long–short portfolio: buy higher-ranked stocks and short lower-ranked stocks.

04◎

Test real-world value

Assess risk-adjusted returns, turnover, trading costs and performance across different market conditions.

The models were evaluated on out-of-sample data to reduce the risk of judging them only by how well they fit historical training data.

FINDING 01

More information is not always better

Adding more company characteristics did not consistently improve performance. A smaller, carefully selected set of signals could work better.

FINDING 02

A good prediction must survive trading costs

The leading strategy's Sharpe ratio fell from about 1.85 before costs to about 1.65 at a 50-basis-point cost assumption.

05 / THE TAKEAWAY

Better investing takes more than a clever algorithm.

This project showed that machine learning can uncover useful stock-selection signals, but the best approach is not necessarily the most complex or the one with the most input variables.

For me, the key lesson was to connect statistical modelling with the questions investors actually face: Is the strategy profitable after costs? How much risk does it take? And can we explain why it works?

OPTIONAL READING
Which models did you compare?

LASSO is a regularised linear model that can shrink less useful coefficients. Random Forest combines many decision trees. XGBoost builds trees sequentially to correct earlier errors. Each was tested across five feature sets (Core20, Core40, Core60, Core80 and Core100), producing 15 combinations.

How did you measure investment value?

I ranked stocks by predicted returns, formed an equally weighted top-minus-bottom decile long–short portfolio, and evaluated annualised returns, volatility, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown and turnover. Trading cost assumptions included 10, 25 and 50 basis points.

How did you guard against overfitting?

The research separated model development from out-of-sample evaluation using historical training, validation and test periods. It also compared performance across feature-set sizes and market regimes. Historical backtests still carry data, implementation and regime-change risks.

What were the main limitations?

Results depend on the historical sample, data quality, rebalancing assumptions and transaction cost estimates. The bear-market subsample was short. Backtested returns should not be interpreted as guaranteed or directly investable returns.
